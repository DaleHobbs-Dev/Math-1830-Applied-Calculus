Generative AI Policy — Category 2

This course encourages you to use AI to learn, not to shortcut learning. AI tools and resources are allowed with instructor approval on specific work inside this course.

College Categories

  • Category 1: AI tools are not allowed.
  • Category 2: AI may be used on some assignments with instructor approval. This course uses Category 2.
  • Category 3: AI tools encouraged across coursework.

This course = Category 2

You may use generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity) for approved tasks. Use it to understand and practice—not to copy final answers. If you are unsure about being able to use AI on a specific task, please ask first.

Healthy AI Use: Principles

  • Ask for hints, not answers. Prefer prompts like “Give me a first step or hint” over “Solve this for me.”
  • Verify and show your work. Always check AI‑generated math, numbers, and citations as AI can be wrong upon occasion. Make sure to write your own steps and explanations in your notebooks so that you have access to those for studying and to use on open-note assessments.
  • Disclose and cite AI use. Briefly state where you used AI and how it changed your work. See the disclosure format below.
  • Protect privacy. Do not paste personal data, student work, tests/quizzes, or copyrighted solutions into public AI tools.
Using AI Responsibly: Before you submit or post work, ask yourself — “Do I truly understand what I’m writing?” If the answer is “no” or even “maybe”, you are likely not using AI in the intended way. Slow down, review, and make sure the ideas are yours.

Discussion Board Expectations

Not allowed

Do not paste the full discussion prompt into an LLM and post the response! It is considered academic dishonesty and instructors want to see what you know and understand, not what AI knows.

Do this instead

Ask targeted questions (e.g., “What is a good real‑world example of marginal cost?” or “Give me a hint for differentiating an exponential with a product.”). Then write a few sentences in your own words about what you learned and how it connects to the unit.

Quick Reference

Required Disclosure

When you use AI on approved tasks, include a one‑line disclosure at the end of your work:

AI Use: Consulted [Tool name] to get a first-step hint and examples; I verified the math and wrote the final explanation myself.

Scenarios: Good vs. Not OK

A. Discussion Post about Elasticity

Good

You ask: “Give me two real‑world examples of price elasticity and a question I could ask classmates.” You then write a paragraph in your voice explaining elasticity with one example, and you pose a question that invites peer responses. You add: AI Use: brainstormed examples; revised and verified.

Not OK

You paste the entire prompt into an LLM and post its answer with minimal to no edits.

B. Solving: Optimize Revenue

Good

You ask: “Give me a hint for the first step to maximize R(x) = x · p(x) when p(x) is linear.” You then compute the derivative R'(x), find the critical point, justify with a sign chart/second derivative, and present your solution. Disclosure included.

Not OK

You submit the LLM’s full derivation as your own or upload the exact problem statement to get a complete solution.

C. Writing: Summary of a Video Lesson

Good

You paste a copy of some of your own notes into AI and then ask AI to outline main ideas from your own notes, then you craft a 150‑word summary in your own personal style. You double‑check definitions and add an example you worked yourself. You include a disclosure like: AI Use: Used AI to summarize my notes; I verified the content and added my own example.

Not OK

You upload the video transcript and submit a lightly edited AI summary.

D. Citations & Sources

Good

You use AI to locate ideas, then you find and cite the original textbook/page or credible source yourself. You do not cite an LLM as a primary source for facts.

Not OK

You present AI‑invented references (“hallucinated” citations) or cite an LLM as if it were a peer‑reviewed source.

If You’re Unsure

When in doubt just feel free to ask your instructor. Unapproved AI use on graded work may be treated as academic misconduct in several instances. Your instructor is always available to review a draft prompt or suggest better ways to use AI productively.
