Course Description

A survey of differential and integral calculus with business applications. Topics include the concepts of limits, the derivative, differentiation techniques, exponential and logarithmic functions, integration, and applications of differentiation and integration. This course is for students not planning to major in engineering or mathematics. This course will fulfill the general education mathematics requirement.

Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs)

  • Determine the slope of a curve at a given point and interpret the result as an instantaneous rate of change.
  • Calculate the slope of a line and interpret its meaning in linear models of business and economics.
  • Evaluate limits algebraically, graphically, and numerically, including those at infinity, to analyze functional behavior and asymptotic trends.
  • Differentiate functions to find first and second derivatives and use these derivatives to describe changes in applied models.
  • Compute derivatives of algebraic, exponential, and logarithmic functions using rules such as power, product, quotient, and chain, including applications of implicit differentiation.
  • Solve applications involving rates of change, such as marginal cost, revenue, or profit.
  • Determine continuity of functions and recognize implications for business modeling (e.g., cost or demand functions).
  • Use derivatives for curve sketching: determine increasing/decreasing behavior, concavity, inflection points, and construct graphs to model applied situations.
  • Analyze and solve optimization problems relevant to business, such as maximizing profit or minimizing cost.
  • Find antiderivatives and indefinite integrals for algebraic, exponential, and logarithmic functions using basic techniques such as u-substitution and apply them to problems of accumulation.
  • Evaluate definite integrals, interpreting results in terms of net change or total value.
  • Apply integration techniques to real-world business/economic problems, such as area between curves or total change over intervals.
  • Solve applied problems involving exponential and logarithmic functions, including growth, decay, and financial models.
  • Differentiate and integrate exponential and logarithmic functions and interpret the results in applied settings.
  • Use appropriate technology tools (graphing calculators or software) to support problem solving and analysis.
  • Communicate mathematical solutions clearly and accurately, demonstrating step-by-step reasoning, and interpret results within real business and economic contexts.

Academic Honesty Expectations

Academic honesty is essential to maintaining integrity in the classroom. Cheating and fabrication are forms of dishonesty that will not be tolerated. Such actions related to any academic exercise, assignment, evaluation, documentation, or assessment may result in a failing grade for the assignment or the entire course.

  • Fabrication: Falsifying or inventing information.
  • Cheating: Using unauthorized materials or aids, borrowing from others’ work, or allowing another to use your work on assignments meant to be completed independently.
  • Plagiarism: Submitting work as your own when it contains borrowed ideas, organization, wording, or other elements from another source without proper acknowledgment.

Working collaboratively with classmates or tutors is acceptable; however, all assignments, projects, and assessments must represent your own understanding and self-expression. If you cannot explain or discuss the work you submitted, it may be considered plagiarized.

Consequences: Plagiarism or academic dishonesty may result in a failing grade on the assignment or the entire course, depending on the intent and severity (ranging from careless citation errors to deliberate copying). These offenses are serious and undermine your own learning.
